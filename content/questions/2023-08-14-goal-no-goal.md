It can be confusing. But it can also be resolved. The quotes aren't actually anti-meditation. But you also need to have a clear and broad definition of meditation to understand how they're not contradictions. I'm not suggesting you stretch the definition of meditation, just realize that it's not just an act you do when you sit. Sitting meditation is where you practice stilling your mind and entering into the present moment and situation you find yourself in. As my Zen teacher says, if that's the only time you practice, you're not going to get much out of it. My teacher's teacher, Zen Master Seung Sahn, would encourage his students to "keep a non-moving mind moment to moment." That's meditation, even though it's not sitting meditation.

The other thing that will help resolve some of these paradoxes is the realization that when you're truly still (sitting or not) your mind isn't preoccupied with how well you're sitting. Seung Sahn has a book called *Wanting Enlightenment is a Big Mistake* which is a collection of talks, including one with that title. To me this points to one of the big paradoxes in meditation: you can get fixated (attached) to how your meditation is going. You can create a goal when you meditate that says "I'm going to get from here to there, with my experience." It's an unconscious goal. But while you're holding this goal in your mind, your mind is full. In my school we sometimes call it checking mind or we call it attachment to body or sitting.

Meditation actually doesn't have a goal. When you've shed all your goals, you're just sitting there, or just walking, or just watching TV. That's Zen. Brad Warner has a chapter in his book *Sit Down and Shut Up* called Goal/No Goal about this and it's a major, major lesson. When I sit to meditate, I constantly come back to this realization and am able to let go when I stop trying to get somewhere and just accept and observe where I am. So there is a goal, kinda, but there also isn't a goal. Hence the name of the chapter.
