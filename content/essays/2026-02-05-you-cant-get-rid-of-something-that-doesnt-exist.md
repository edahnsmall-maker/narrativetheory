Like so many other "spiritual" seekers, Alan Watts turned me onto Zen as a teen. Zen was always mysterious (a part of its marketing genius) but Watts was able to make a little sense out of the mystery, even when it wasn't supposed to make sense.

Watts makes 2 important points about the ego:

1. You can't dismantle something that doesn't exist.
2. Trying to dismantle can often be another tactic that strengthens the illusion of ego.

Let's break it down.

1. When we talk about the ego, what do we really mean? We mean the sense of a separate, continuous "me." When you say "I want to get something to eat" it's the thing you think is wanting something. It's not that none of you exists—you still hear, you taste, you have thoughts and memories. It's that there's no separate "you" from those things. It (the object, e.g., sounds) doesn't happen to a you (a separate subject). They're not separate. The inside and outside are the same thing. And everything is always changing. There's nothing stable to call a "me" or an "I." Everything is empty of an inherent identity. It's a composite made up of everything else. So if you're trying to dismantle this ego thing, you're going to run into a problem because it ultimately doesn't exist in the form you think it exists in. It's like trying to dismantle something that's already dismantled.
2. Watts's second point is that trying to dismantle something like that can sometimes be another one of the ego's tactics. When we say "ego" here it's not in the sense of a separate self (remember, that doesn't exist), but in the delusion we carry around. The separate-self delusion is always looking for something. Its nature is to desire, resist, and create conflict and malaise. It's never really satisfied because there's always more. Enlightenment and the process we think will lead us there can just become another way for the mind to keep resisting, keep looking for more, keep distracting itself.

So should we just stop?

Stop searching but don't stop practicing. To practice IS to stop searching. To practice is to focus the mind and let it grind to a halt, not to become blank and brainless, but to open up to what's always here: this moment in all its ordinariness and gloriousness, its power and humility, its volume and silence. When we practice we entertain the notion that nothing is missing, not even enlightenment. We see what it's like to live that way, and what it does to our hearts and bodies. We see that compassion and kindness is the only thing that truly ever makes sense.
