Now it's incredibly important to completely see through words. We can use words to get confused and circular very quickly, labeling thoughts as "angry" or "scared" or "sad." But we need to be careful about our nomenclature here.

To think about thoughts, we need to think of them as associations over time. One thing triggers the next thing. But "thing" is not just one thing at a time, necessarily. It's really going from one state to one state as it builds a narrative about the world.

But we just warned about language, so how do we go deeper?

Let's do a thought experiment where we have different people seeing a mom feeding her baby on a public bench. The mother's breast is concealed, but you can see the baby.

Now let's pretend we had a machine to look into people's bodies and thoughts and current state when they glimpse this mother.

We will of course find very different associations that these individuals have, and as these associations elaborate, the narrative will gain more and more density.

One might be surprised. One turned on. One upset. One delighted.

But these are words. The turned-on person associated the scene with sexual pleasure and feels something in their body. The upset person automatically projects hostility and outgroups the mother. The delighted person might see their body go into a state of relaxation or warmth, and smile and hold that state of delight. They have positive associations.

So, while we can use words to abstractly describe states and content, the best way to understand it is to look through it, beyond the words, and think about what is happening in the thoughts and body (in the mind, in experience).

All of these people are reacting consistent with the state of mind they were in a split second before seeing this mother. That is to say, they are seeing consistent with their mode.

But let's go a step further and think about the same person looking at the same mother, but this time we can rewind the video and activate a different state in the same person. This person will obviously also have different reaction-states to the same stimuli. This isn't so hard to understand. Sometimes you see something and you can't stand it. Other times it's the greatest thing in the world. For example, think about a partner's idiosyncrasies. Sometimes they drive you mad. Other times they're adorable.

We're constantly cycling through different modes. And these modes also determine how we think about ourselves. And our loved ones. The future, and our immediate future. The past and who we are. Our value. Our identity.
