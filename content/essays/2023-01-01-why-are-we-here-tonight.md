Why are we here tonight? There are personal reasons, and bigger reasons that appear with time.

The personal reasons are infinite. For me it was a curiosity about Zen and why it didn't seem to make sense, but yet seemed to make perfect sense to some people. It was also at some point about overcoming shyness and self-criticism and judgment.

But the bigger reason is that practice helps you see more clearly and act with clarity and confidence in a way that you know is right in your heart. In our school we call it correct function, which is really about finding ways to help situations and people find peace and happiness. We have a formula we use, correct situation, relationship, and function, the idea being that practice helps you see situations clearly, your relationship to them, and ultimately how to function: what to do, how to respond.

I used to think this was a discrete process and even a chronological one, separated into steps. I'm gonna see it, understand it, consider my role, then respond. I don't think that anymore. The process, in my experience, is all mixed together and happens almost all at once. As I respond I also make new situations, and then respond to those. It's not an active state of mind you force yourself into either. In fact, force is maybe the one thing that's not involved.

I'm going to go into that in a second, but this idea of joining the world and then having a compass that helps guide you, it's not just about being a good human. It's not a reward you get for doing right or fighting for justice. It's not because you belong to a group and want more belonging. You're doing what's natural and where your heart and mind steer you. That's the big picture. That's the reason we practice. Not to sit and gain things in a room that's secluded, but to take that energy out and mix it into the world.

So how do we do this? Why aren't we doing this all the time?

The answer has to do with the way our thinking and needs, our desires, fears, and goals, all can take over our mind and affect the way we see things. It's not magical, not esoteric. You can experience it yourself and probably have. When you're activated and upset, say someone really got you angry, and it's someone you have a history of getting into conflict with... at that moment, the way you see that situation is not the same as when you take a little while to calm down. Maybe a walk, maybe a distraction. Maybe a talk with someone. The grip of thinking and the mental world it activates (interpretations, evaluations of things), and the responses or functions that that mental world provokes, are all different in quality after you've calmed down. Our angry mind or our fearful mind generates the world we live in.

What we do in meditation is start to weaken that grip of the mind. We go from *being* our thoughts to *having* them. That means we go from experiencing our thoughts as us, and our ideas about the world and others as real, to having thoughts that don't command our attention the same way but just exist.

When I started meditating I had some powerful experiences right away. I spent a lot of time trying to replicate that experience and actually had some success, but ultimately discovered what was really going on. And I encourage you to do the same and investigate your own experience and outcomes, like a scientist in a lab.

What I started to realize is that it was seeking, grasping, wanting, or just goals that were the factor that really affected my meditation quality. It's a lesson I keep having to relearn and reinforce every time I sit.

Goals can creep into your practice without you knowing. It's natural, because every other task in our life has a goal we use to measure our progress. But meditation is different, because it's goals that are the subject of the meditation experience itself.

When I sit I will almost without fail set up a goal about what this meditation is supposed to be. That leads immediately to a framework for judging the quality of meditation against that goal. In my mind I'm planning to go from A to B, from my current experience to a meditation mind, and any deviation from that is frustrating.

But something helped me stop doing that. After a retreat one year and lots of frustrations, the chukpi was struck, the practice session was over, and suddenly I was able to relax. Really relax. It was quiet.

I think of meditation as calming the energy that animates the body and mind. When that energy is calmed and comes to rest, the thoughts can still come, but you aren't fighting them anymore and they don't command your attention the same way. You have them and they exist. And then they don't. And you're just here, sitting with a slightly opening heart, with full attention.

A mantra or a focus on breathing can help you stabilize this energy so it's not all over the place and activating your mind and that default resistance. Once it starts to become ordered, I often find that everything else starts to drop off by itself, to be quite honest. Even the goal of meditation as an exercise can dissolve, so it's just you and your experience. Or maybe that's the same thing.

So over and over we just relax and see what it's like, to be here and to stay here, even if we have thoughts, whatever it is. We just sit here. There's no place we go, because we're not travelling anywhere but our current experience. That way we start to transform and release that energy we have that's always hungry and looking for something more, or sometimes something less, but it's always in a fight with this experience and always monitoring progress, measuring this moment against a model moment, and generating all sorts of feelings, good and bad, based on this measurement.

All that energy gets starved and this natural peace just starts to shine through, embracing things easily and naturally.

There isn't much we have to do. You don't have to manufacture and force your experience into this space. In fact, it won't really work, because it's more argument and measurement and trying. Stop trying to fight this experience. Let it be. Don't believe that it's not right, or it's not meditation. There's nothing wrong with it at all. That's where you find peace, a peace that you can sense is valuable and worth sharing with others. That's why we practice. That's how we practice.
