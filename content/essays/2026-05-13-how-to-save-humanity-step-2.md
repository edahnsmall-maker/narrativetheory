Step 1 was humanizing the people we've started to hate, whether that's family members, partners, political adversaries, rival nations and leaders, religious groups, etc., etc. That's something that starts to happen naturally as we put down all of our thinking and resentment and try to see things fresh. That's not the same as being blank and oblivious: there is still hatred, greed, delusion, and suffering in the world. But putting down our thinking helps us see things fresh. Maybe we start to see things differently—new angles, new opportunities, new possibilities, and the courage to act from kindness and wisdom starts to grow by itself.

Easy! We're already doing it every time we try to wake up from the dream and see what's real, what's truth. The Sun is shining, the floor is brown, the trees are green.

Step 2 is how we participate in the world.

Storytime: When I was young, like a lot of people, I wanted to change the world dramatically (or move to a commune, which, if you're interested, hit me up). I had big ideas and ambitions.

When I was 28ish and still figuring out where and how to practice, I went to stay at a Thai monastery for 2 nights in Temecula. It was interesting. Everyone was very happy. There were at least 2 other ex-lawyers who had decided to leave their jobs and become monks. All in all there were probably less than 50 people there.

I wondered why this wasn't a huge movement. Why weren't there more people, and why weren't they trying to advertise this thing and grow as fast as possible? This was the answer! Not only were they not doing that, but to join them, you first had to live outside the monastery for a year. A YEAR. Talk about barriers to entry, amirite?

But it dawned on me that they had a different strategy. It wasn't to grow and expand. Those types of movements burn bright but soon fizzle out, usually amidst some juicy scandal. (There are like 15 Netflix docs devoted to this phenomenon.)

The monastery's strategy, instead, was to focus more on the inside (the people in the community) than the outside (the marketing and growth). They were creating something that was small but strong, and less likely to fizzle out.

There are two lessons here:

1. In order to change things, we have to develop the inside first. That means putting in the time and doing the work of Zen practice: chanting, bowing, sitting, watching our mind moment-to-moment, taming our passions, letting go of anger and delusion, and trying our best to be kind and patient. If we don't, we run the risk of acting unskillfully, adding more problems than we're alleviating, empowered (and encumbered) by a sense of self-righteousness.
2. You don't have to make big grand gestures to help the world. Every interaction you have throughout your day is a chance to help a little bit. Your kindness, clarity, understanding, and patience reverberate in the space around you. Big gestures and small. Your kindness touches others, who pass that kindness to others in some form, at some time. Like the Thai monastery, this kind of practice is sustainable and harder to break once it becomes a habit. It's how we fulfill our Bodhisattva vow to save all beings.
