(Control alt delete are the keys you press to restart a PC)

I encounter a lot of people who say they can't meditate, and as a dharma teacher, I try to help them sort it out. I think we all struggle with meditation because we're working against a habit of thinking that's been cultivated our entire life. But over time, with guidance and experimentation, you get better at understanding the terrain of meditation and how to navigate it.

What most people say is that they just can't get their mind to calm down. It's probably a familiar experience to everyone reading this. You sit and you try to still your mind or focus it, and it's just all over the place. You start getting a little frustrated. Maybe you think something is wrong with you personally…

Why can't I sit still like that guy across from me? Is he even moving? Wait, is he okay? Jeez, I wonder what his life is like. He probably works in a spa or a garden, or a garden spa. Dammit I'm thinking again! Maybe I need to try something else like yoga.

## CONTROL

But the problem here isn't the thinking, and the solution isn't to force the thinking to stop. Thinking comes and goes.

The problem is the fighting of it (i.e., the attachment to thinking). The problem is Control. Let me explain.

We start every meditation session with the same attitude we have towards everything in our lives: get from A to B. A is the start, B is the goal. Anything less than B needs improvement. A is rejected. We work to get closer and closer to the goal.

That same template gets repurposed for meditation. "I'm going to get from A [a busy mind] to B [a calm serene mind]." But we're just playing the control game. The control game is desire. The control game is built from attachment to outcomes, states, feelings.

What we don't realize is that the conflict with our experience—the busy mind, the starting point, what IS—is what generates the friction and frustration. The reason we don't feel peace is because we're really in a state of war with ourselves and our experience.

## ALT

So what can you do? What can you do that isn't just another expression of war and rejection? If you do nothing, you stay frustrated. If you try to fix it, you're just engaging in more conflict. It's like a man hanging from a tree by his mouth, arms and legs bound, who needs to explain what Zen is. If he acts, he falls. If he doesn't act, he fails at his task. (This is a famous kong-an that we study.)

In Zen we explore the alternative (Alt) to control: what happens if I stop trying to fight this? What if I just look? What if I make peace with it right now? Then what? What's it like right now to be bothered, messy, imperfect, without trying to escape it, control it, fix it, define it, hold onto it…without doing anything fancy? What if I just let everything be, just now, as it already is? What if I stop doing?

## DELETE

When we try that out, we begin to notice a change. It's not that our mind suddenly becomes still, but the conflict with it softens. As soon as we stop fighting what's happening and open up to it, we become present. Right away. And the suffering that lives in the little gap between reality and what we want reality to be vanishes. Like it's been deleted.

When we're no longer convulsing against life, we're naturally more available to it. More available to this breath, this moment, this world, and the people right in front of us. Our thoughts no longer have the same power over our perception. Like a giant reset occurs. And a softness starts to emerge on its own—a softness that was always there.

Control, alt, delete, and reset.
