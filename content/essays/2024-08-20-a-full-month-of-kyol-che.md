I don't think people quite know what a full month of Kyol Che (our biannual retreat) really means and what it does to you.

I'm not going to lie: it's tough. It's tough to be away from your family and friends and the excitement and freedom of everyday life. It's tough on your body as well. Bowing, sitting, more sitting, even more sitting, the heat, the humidity. And then there's the challenge of wrestling with (or watching) the parade that is your mind. It seems like at times it'll never stop harassing you with distractions, doubts, fantasies, and expectations.

But this is a practice for the courageous. And great courage means that we meet great challenges with integrity and determination. You start to find ways to manage the pain. The pain of missing loved ones starts to soften. And the mind starts to unwind a little bit, together with some of the fantasies, goals, and expectations it whips together.

I'm on an airplane now flying home, and the college student next to me asked what I was doing in Providence. I told him I was at a meditation retreat, and he said, "That must have been…" and he trailed off. I said, "Boring?" and smiled. He said, "Yeah," and I said, "Actually, there's a lot that happens in your mind."

Which is partially true. Because what ends up happening is less activity: less thinking, less striving, less conflict with what already is happening. But in that space, that void, a lot of changes happen that seem novel. Maybe they're not truly novel since they've been there all along; we're just seeing them for the first time.

The first week I call the energy-settling week. Your energy and body start to calm down. It's less agitated but still active, so your thinking takes on a different quality: active but softer. Not combative, but still wandering, curious, exploring the changes in experience that are starting to root.

The second week is where I start to feel deep stillness. It's not permanent, but it can stretch hours or days. In those moments, even the goal or idea of meditation dissolves. You're just sitting there. You realize how rare it is that you can just sit in silence. Your mind is no longer attacking itself. It's a taste of "no attainment with nothing to attain," as the Heart Sutra says. And you can see how automatically and habitually our minds generate all sorts of conflict, not unlike the conflicts we see between couples, tribes, and countries. It shares the same qualities: it's war. It's not being satisfied with what you have, thinking something is always missing. Where does that come from? Why is it always there?

We engage in wars constantly: wars with the moment, wars with ourselves, wars with our situations, wars between "us" and "them." It's so pervasive we don't even notice it. But sitting in silence puts that mind-reflex into sharp focus.

Throughout the retreat, there's a push-and-pull dynamic, at least for me. You discover something that you know is important, then you try to return there and you fail miserably. Then you reach a point of exhaustion or demoralization, which is followed by another round of inner silence, intimacy, and peace, because you keep forgetting that the activity that led to the silence in the first place wasn't striving but letting go. It's a lesson you learn weekly if not daily.

With long bouts of meditation, our habit of thinking from the perspective of our self starts to really weaken, which sets the stage for big insights: seeing into your self and its components, really exploring that question *what am I?* or *what is this?* in a deep, determined, and focused manner. You're like a dog that's bit down and won't let go. You can pop out of your mind for a while to see reality as it is. You start to wake up. You're just here. Just walking. Just eating. Just being. Is a big insight guaranteed? Of course not, but persistence pays off.

When you're sitting for long periods of time, even a couple of days, your heart begins to break. It's painful to experience the sadness and toxicity of the world, and equally painful to meet it naturally with love. This is the great love and great sadness that we chant about (dae ja, dae bi), which are really one thing. It's already there inside all of us. We can reflect that love back onto ourselves and heal from the toxicity, disappointments, and false narratives that we've accumulated about ourselves over the years. I'm talking about trauma and even things that might not be considered trauma: the mind-habits we pick up, the harsh way we view ourselves, the assumptions that are embedded in our perception that steer our interactions, decisions, and outcomes in a way that feels mysterious and uncontrollable.

The insights you get on retreat can have big consequences on your life because they go to the heart of the human condition. Questions like: What is real and what is just my thinking? What's important? What does it mean to be present? What does it mean to live well? Am I my thoughts or something else? Is there another way to live? What does it mean to have peace? What does it mean to be vulnerable? What does that have to do with feeling connection?

You're reading this because you're already interested in those questions (or you're my family member and I forced you to read this, in which case, yo!). There are answers, but it takes persistence and ferocity, as well as patience and kindness. If you can go to Kyol Che, go. The longer, the better. Challenge yourself to do a full month and then brag about it afterwards. Or write an essay.
