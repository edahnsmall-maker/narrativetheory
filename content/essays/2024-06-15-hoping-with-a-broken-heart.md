I have been struggling lately. The world looks bleak. Situations in my life look bleak. I sometimes think I'm holding in more pain than I can because I'm not sure others can really contain it. Maybe I'm wrong.

Politics, social media, the vitriol, the tribalism and vilification, the ignorance, the delusion, climate change, fake news, AI. It's just like we're on the Titanic and frozen, or pretending like it's not going down.

Countless people have asked after dharma talks: what can we do? And I've heard a lot of… resignation. From teachers at different levels, teachers whose work I take to be gold. "We can't do anything." "Do what you can." And that's got me thinking about my role.

In our school we say in Zen we find our correct situation, relationship, and function. I think we all develop ideas about what this means when we're starting practice. Where does that relationship come from, though? What is it? Is it defined?

I don't think it is. I think we invent our role, and I think it's not defined for us. It's open depending on where we are, where the situation is, and what the needs of the situation are. That's something I wonder if Zen Master Seung Sahn considered when he talked about correct situation, but I wonder if it should be a separate category.

Well, I don't know. Consider it arrogance. Consider it a lifelong commitment to being a contrarian. I don't know if we need to feel despair. I feel it too. But I also feel the impulse to be optimistic. And I'm going to argue that this is actually a part of our practice and a responsibility we have: to have hope, continue the fight, to inspire, and to find a way to help in big ways, not just small ones.

My assumption is this: that hope can affect outcomes. My argument is this, and I will label the assumption so you can appreciate its significance (or lack thereof):

1. Our practice helps build an impulse to help others. That is our collective direction whether we practice or not, but we experience it clearly and consistently when we practice.
2. Helping occurs on many levels, subtle (helping someone cross a street) and profound (a change in life for a person, or a small or large change for many people; see Gandhi).
3. Hope is a way to help. Hope inspires others. And hope may have an effect on outcomes (this is the assumption, but you can see it's not a big part of the argument). Real hope is believing what you say, not just saying it with no courage and conviction.
4. We don't know our roles. We don't know what impact we can have on things. And some of us need to make big impacts, even if we aren't sure we can.
5. Therefore, it's important that we summon as much hope as we can. Because hope itself matters. Genuine hope. In as many situations, subtle and profound, as we can.

I have had my own personal experience with hope and have studied it passively. I study everything passively.

The point is this: we are the practitioners of don't-know. We are the cult of don't-know (in a good way, in the way Alan Watts described Zen as a cult of Buddhism). We are students of a way, and our way points to help, peace, strength, and a reverence for beauty. An impulse to help.

Sometimes help requires optimism and hope, even when you aren't feeling it just yet. I say try it on, like an outfit. What does the world look like? What does your situation look like? Your hopes with your practice? How do your situations change? How does the world respond? How does it meet your energy?

Don't be positive for yourself, be positive for them.
