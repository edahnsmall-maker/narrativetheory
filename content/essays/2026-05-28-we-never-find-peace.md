People often say they can't meditate. I often say no one can meditate, because meditation is not a thing we can "do," like everything else we do.

We use our minds to "do" things: to solve problems, to plan outings, to select gifts, decide where to eat (CJ's or Canter's?).

But what makes Zen meditation ("don't-know mind") what it is—its defining quality—is that it's not a doing at all. It's not something we produce with our mind when we've thought about it long enough. It's not something you get to with the power of your reasoning. It's not something you can hone in on with precision.

Meditation is what happens when all doing comes to an end.

And what is the end of doing?

When you stop trying to get somewhere. Anywhere. Not trying to be Zen. Or calm. Not trying to control your thoughts. Not trying to breathe correctly. When all effort collapses. As our teacher used to say, when you "put it all down."

That's when you come into contact with this moment just as it is, without conditions and distortions. My breath is as it is. My body is as it is. My thinking is as it is. My mood is as it is. Everything is just as it is.

That's when we discover something important: that we don't find inner peace when our mind finally behaves the way we want and our life looks the way we want it to. We make peace when we stop fighting what already is.

And the interesting thing—or at least what I find interesting—is that when we stop fighting with our experience, we stop fighting with other things too. The need to win, punish, advertise, and outshine others comes to a halt. Our cravings move from the driver's seat to the back seat. And as our cravings and needs subside, our suffering dissolves with it. Peace becomes a style of being, not just a personal goal for me. And the Bodhisattva vow to save all beings becomes real as inner peace turns into outer peace.
