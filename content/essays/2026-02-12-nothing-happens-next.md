We use our minds to do everything.

Doing implies 3 things: a starting point, a doing of something (action), and an end result.

[START]——ACTION——>[END]

As we do, we measure the outcomes and adjust how we're doing the thing (the action, the intervention). Anything is like this: driving, getting a job, even having a conversation.

When we come to a spiritual practice, we have the same ideas: that I'm missing something and I need to DO SOMETHING to get from where I am (the start) to where I want to be—some kind of end point that we fantasize about—a state of bliss, of security, of recognition and admiration. We have some kind of idea of what the right meditation looks like to get to this end point.

Then we sit.

And then the judging begins! "It's not this kind of breathing," you think to yourself. "My mind is too noisy." "I don't feel relaxed." "I can't control my focus."

We think we're going to strategize our way to the end state. But Zen (along with dancing and music) is one of the few things that CAN'T work that way, because Zen is defined by the letting go of that habit of trying to get somewhere.

In Zen we just ask ourselves: what happens if I stop all that DOING? What if I don't fight this? What if I don't give myself a job? Everything becomes clear. It's just as it is. The thinking, the body, the breathing, the silence, the floor, the awareness of being in the room. And soon that fades, because it all fades, and silence begins to fill the space.

As this experience comes sharper into focus, we can examine it carefully. What is it? What is its relationship to me? Where is the "me" right now?

When we stop waiting for something to happen, we're just better able to inhabit this moment.
