Today's talk is about conflict. I want to share some observations and a way to think about conflict.

Take a second and think about heated conflict. Generally speaking, conflict always feels the same.

From the inside, conflict feels all-absorbing. Tense. Oppositional. Me vs. you.

From the outside, or once we've calmed down, conflict always has certain signature features. And it's clear and easier to see in the system of Zen or meditation.

What happens? We jump into our minds. We jump into sides. You vs. me. And the person who's the object of our conflict becomes enemy #1, right? Whether it's a friend, a coworker, a lover, political opponents, we develop a story about this person that's very narrow. And it's all mind generated.

Is it real?

It feels real. But once the gloves come off, once we've calmed down, we see that it's all bullshit. That what we thought about the person was in our head.

It's the same thing that goes on in meditation.

In our school we learn how to divest from our narratives about the world and situations and see them for what they're worth.

One of the things I realized on retreat was argument. While I always knew I enjoyed arguing with ideas for sport, it wasn't until retreat that I realized there's a subtle level of argument that we have with our situation. We don't just accept it. We argue with it. We say it's not okay. We label it and then squirm away from it. We try to strategize around it. If it's anxiety or anger or frustration that we feel, we think about how to move over it. Often it's just a perception of us being subordinate to others.

But there's an alternative, which is just listening and watching. Seeing the body, seeing the mind do its thing. And staying with our experience without trying too hard.

It's the same experience that we have in meditation.

We can go down the path of conflict, looking at our meditation and saying "that's not correct meditation. I'm not doing it right. My mind is this and that. I can't focus." We call it checking mind, but it's argument with what is.

In Zen we ask: how is this just now? How is it already, when I sat down to meditate?

How is it right now, listening to this talk?

It's this same technique of listening and watching (or surrender) that paradoxically gives us options. As our experience starts to unfold in real time, if we can hang with it and look at it without criticizing it, without pushing it away, without holding onto it, and without getting into the drama and narrative, sticking with the present-moment body changes and shifts, we can see the experiences unfolding and look beneath them, and we're able to see more information.

We aren't just looking at the typical sides (you vs. me) and the anger we feel and the narrative we develop. We can see that underneath everything there's a vulnerability and fragility and tenderness that calls for a different reaction or response. New information, new output. Maybe instead of getting angry we explain what's happening and find a way to make peace on the outside that mirrors the peace on the inside.

This is how we slowly bring peace into the world, situation by situation, moment by moment. And it really starts on the cushion, by being able to say "what is this?" Or in this very moment, by asking: what's this like, without getting into the stories, just feeling this body, these shoulders, these eyes, this breath, and how it all moves together. Coming into focus more and more with each breath and each moment.

The Zen way isn't pretending. It isn't manufacturing states of mind or states of bliss. It's the ordinary mind: not fighting, not forcing, not pushing, not holding, not getting into the stories. Just as it is. This is don't know mind. This is what we practice.

Breathing in, clear mind. Breathing out, don't know. Buddha synthesized Buddhism into four truths, but our Zen Master beat him and summarized practice into one phrase: only go straight, don't know.

Only don't know.

I hope everyone continues to practice, attain enlightenment, and save all beings.
