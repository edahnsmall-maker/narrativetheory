I sometimes like to ask teachers how their meditation has changed over the years. And I sometimes think about how my meditation has changed. The common denominator seems to be "doing less."

The first few minutes of sitting are always the same for me. I sit with an idea of what I'm supposed to be experiencing. There's a hidden idea I harbor about what meditation is supposed to look like. And feel like. It looks calm. It feels intimate. It feels slowed down.

"Okay, let's slow it down."

"Let's breathe deep."

"Let's try and calm this mind down."

All of this is doing something. I'm going to get from here to there. Here is bad, there is good. It's what we sometimes call "opposites thinking." It's a subtle form of conflict with what ALREADY IS, in favor of what we desire and crave.

I think we all do this. I do it every time I sit.

But after a few minutes, something starts to happen on its own: we get tired of fighting. The mind starts to operate slightly differently. It's a little more concentrated, a little smoother, a little less jagged. And we start to open up to how things are, without the resentment. We start to simply notice. And we start the process of waking up.

I sometimes think about this as widening a target. We start with a narrow target of what we're supposed to feel and experience, but with time and patience, the target starts to widen until it contains whatever we're experiencing right now. The target becomes whatever we're experiencing.

Then the target disappears completely. No attainment, with nothing to attain, as the Heart Sutra says.

This is why talking about Zen is so tricky. Because at the end you realize you didn't actually do anything. You stopped trying to get anywhere and finally stood still.
