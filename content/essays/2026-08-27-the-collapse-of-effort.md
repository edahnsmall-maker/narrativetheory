We all come to Zen practice with some hope that we can change something about our life, right? How we feel, how we interact, the results we get, whatever. We develop a private strategy for ourselves, that with the right technique and right effort, we can experience a life-changing event that'll solve all our problems. An enlightenment event that'll flip a switch…finally. And when we sit to meditate, we put this private strategy to work.

With a little reflection, though, we start to see that the private strategy is just craving dressed in spiritual clothing. Yes, the same craving that's the cause of suffering. All it takes is a little attention and curiosity.

When we sit with a goal and strategy, we're really just thinking. We get locked into a cycle of observing our experience, evaluating it as good or bad, modifying our technique, and then back to observing if there's a change. We call it "checking mind" in our school, like compulsively checking to see if your rice is cooking.

The old Zen Masters knew about this trap and invented clever ways to draw their students out of it:

Ma Jo (the famous Mazu) was a young monk who spent nearly all his time sitting meditation. One day his teacher, Zen Master Nam Ak, asked him:

"What are you doing?"

Ma Jo said, "I'm practicing sitting Zen."

"Why?"

"I want to become a Buddha."

Nam Ak picked up a roof tile and began rubbing it against a stone.

After a while Ma Jo asked, "What are you doing?"

Nam Ak said, "I'm polishing this tile to make a mirror."

Ma Jo said, "How can polishing a tile make it into a mirror?"

Nam Ak replied, "How can sitting meditation make you into a Buddha?"

Nam Ak was challenging Ma Jo's idea that he could use his meditation technique to transform himself. He was helping him give up on his private strategy and just sit, listen. To stop chasing, judging, and interfering.

Ironically, disappointment in our spiritual journey can have the same effect. It can make us give up on our private hopes of transforming, sometimes in a profound way—a total collapse of effort—and pay attention to what's already here. It doesn't need to be a great disappointment; even small moments of fatigue can lead to gaps in craving.

I'm not suggesting we all go and plan big disappointments, which is about as rational as planning your own surprise party. But I'm suggesting we pay attention to our motivation in practice and see if we're secretly chasing something—a state, enlightenment, our idea of good meditation.

Just focusing on something steady like our breath can help ease that chasing habit with a little patience and care. It doesn't have to be complicated. That experience—of no longer chasing—can feel disorienting at first as we start to emerge from our thoughts and settle into the silence behind it all. But soon we start to find our balance and we see that the whole point wasn't to get enlightenment, but to realize there was nothing to get in the first place.
