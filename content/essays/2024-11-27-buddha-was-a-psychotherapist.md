I guess you could say I'm a Buddhist. To be honest, I'm not exactly sure what that means, to BE a Buddhist. Do I meditate? Yes. But lots of people do. Do I think Buddha was right about a lot of things? Yes, but I also think Einstein was. Do I belong to a religion or tribe? I do, but I belong to a lot of different groups and tribes. I play drums with a group every Sunday at the beach, but I would call myself a Drum Circlist.

Buddhism doesn't place a lot of demands on its practitioners, which tracks for a religion that talks about peace and compassion and wisdom. You don't have to believe anything, or be born to a certain person, or even go through an intense initiation process. Most religions are interested in exclusivity. They want you all for themselves. Not Buddhism.

What makes it hard to know if you're a Buddhist or not is that it's hard to know what Buddhism really is. It's not a traditional religion like most western religions. And truthfully, I never really saw it that way anyway.

Buddhism always struck me more as a map. It's a map of how to make sense of your mind and your experience, specifically, the experience of struggling, feeling like an alien, being upset, feeling like something's missing. Not only is that the same experience across geographies, groups, and generations, I would even argue it's part of the same single fundamental experience. One experience, lots of shades and dimensions, but still one experience, of agitation and feeling like something's wrong, and fighting against that. In fact, we could even say the entire experience is conflict: conflict with something that's happening or something we're trying to avoid. I say that with confidence because I've experienced my life without that, and that puts it all into perspective.

And that's really the same experience that Buddha had, though likely on a much more profound and permanent level than mine. He deconstructed his experience by sharpening his mind, and noticed that it wasn't what it seemed. The feeling of "himself" fell apart as he realized everything was networked with everything else (my vision is constituted by the outside world, same for sound), and everything was changing and falling apart. He realized that a separate, permanent self couldn't exist by definition. He saw it falling apart. He didn't just understand the words like you're understanding them (or not, okay) right now.

When the idea of himself was recognized as just an idea, all the striving and planning and strategizing and CONFLICT dissolved. I know because it happened to me too. It doesn't make sense to consider yourself separate when you EXPERIENCE everything as connected and your self as an impossibility. And it doesn't make sense to struggle and try to gain for yourself if your self isn't even a thing. It's instant relief.

Buddha saw what he was made of (literally, he called it the five skandhas) and what he wasn't made of (a separate self), and that changed his whole experience. He understood that suffering came from this separate self. He taught people how to deconstruct it methodically, through a system that helped calm your mind and sharpen it to a point.

He studied (through observation) the relationships and processes by which we construct a separate self, start chasing and fighting with things, and end up lost in our own chaotic mental world, peeking out on rare occasions only.

When you read Buddhist literature (especially the early stuff that's attributed directly to him) you see that there's nothing to believe in. Everything he's talking about is experience, how to think about it, how to master it… how to deconstruct it. And then you realize that Buddha was really a psychologist and psychotherapist. And an absolutely incredible one.

He didn't do talk therapy, and didn't ask you how you're feeling. But he did demonstrate compassion and developed a system that could not just heal people, but heal them deeply and steadily. And in Zen you find traces of the same wisdom and insight, but in a different format and presentation.

Ultimately, you can't separate psychology, psychotherapy, philosophy (the interesting stuff, lol), and spirituality. They're all talking about the same thing. That doesn't make them all correct. It's important to scrutinize what you hear. But many of them have more than kernels of truth. And many of those themes and threads have appeared in my own experience and journey… and thinking.
