When you're meditating, you keep running into that challenge of "I know this. I know this is not it," because I'm probably thinking a lot. How do I confront that?

In our school, we tell people, "Just let it go, let it pass." I don't know. My experience is not that. My experience is you keep wrestling with your inability until you stop trying. And then you get a taste of not trying, and then you let that pervade. You let it soak or spread, and you do your best. And when it starts to happen, you start to have these moments where everything is alive. Everything is really alive. Things don't lose their character. They don't become words anymore.

But you feel awake.

There's a story I really like. It's about a monk who goes up to the Zen master. These monks have been practicing for years and years, and he asks him a question like: "What is Buddha? What is enlightenment?" The nature of the question doesn't matter, because the Zen masters always tell you the same answer: wake up right now. Let me show you how to get out of your thinking.

You have to imagine these monks. They've been reading sutras, studying, they have all these ideas about things. Who knows if it was a senior monk or a twenty-year-old? You don't know. But they have all these ideas. And he goes, "Teach me. Show me what that is." And the Zen master walks up to him and kicks him in the chest super hard, and the guy flies back. And he falls, and he starts laughing. Something happened. He just starts laughing and keeps laughing. He discovers something. And he writes a poem saying, "I've been searching for this for years," something like that. What did he experience? The Zen master was telling him the same thing: get out of your head, buddy. It's not in your head.

You can do that with meditation. If you just follow your breath, you can kind of get it. But that never worked for me. For me, it's always trying, frustration, and then the end of trying after waiting.

And that's what I want to give my Dharma talk on: the end of trying. You could call it surrender, but I like "the end of trying," because that's mine. Everybody should have their own words and their own languages.

I don't really think about what it means that much, to be honest, because thinking about what it means is the trap.

When you're thinking, you have a goal and a strategy, and as long as you're doing that your mind is always active and it's always missing what's in front of you. You're never going to get anywhere except what's in front of you right now. And then you ask that question, "What is the relationship between I and what's in front of me?", which eventually happens anyway. In our school we point to it with the question, "What am I? What is this?" Why are those the same questions? Interesting clue I just threw out there.

"What am I? What is this?" It's always right in front of you. Where am I in this relationship? Where does "me" end and "that" start? Everything outside me, everything I see right now: is it I who see, or is there just seeing without an owner?

You can't answer it conceptually. And we don't say it like that in our school because it triggers people's minds. It triggers your mind: "How do I see it this way? How do I think about it? How do I have it?" If you really want to get to the bottom of that question, the only way is to get intimate with it. When you go into mind-space, you're missing fundamental parts of the answer. You have to answer with your own experience. That pulls the whole thing along. The answer comes from looking at your experience, not conceptualizing. Anyone can conceptualize. Books and books and books, that's all conceptualization. The one thing you can't conceptualize is a pinch. You can't conceptualize that.

You have to do the same thing with that question in meditation. Meditation is your vehicle. In some ways it's the end of the whole thing, because after you see there is no separate self, you're awakened. And when you're awakening, you're not trying anymore. There's nothing to try for anymore. The whole thing made no sense. The whole life of trying makes no sense. It just falls apart when you see there is no separate "me."

And that's the practice. That's your whole Zen in a nutshell.

We try to emerge with force and it never works. We try to emerge by controlling and that's the problem.

Don't make that into a concept to strive towards. Just listen and go beyond intention.

That's not a dumb place. It's actually very clear and present. And simple too. And you can wake up into it. That's what the guy realized when he was laughing. That's what Buddha, awakened, means.
