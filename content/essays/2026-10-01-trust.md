You could say Zen is ultimately about trust.

But the way we normally live our lives isn't through trust but force. We have an idea that if we apply enough effort, we're going to be able to get what we need—what we think is missing—and our lives will finally be satisfying.

That can show up in our everyday lives and in Zen practice too. In everyday life, we might think we have to solve all of our problems before we can finally settle. And as Zen students we can smuggle in that same way of thinking. We have an idea of how we're going to change ourselves with practice. It shows up in big ways and in small ways too, when we struggle to make our meditation "right" or look like some ideal state. It's the same thing being expressed in different domains: something has to change before we can be complete.

That desperation to fix something, to become complete, and to change things urgently is what the Buddha was talking about when he talked about craving.

The Zen approach is different. It says "forget trying to fix and arrange everything because that doesn't really lead to satisfaction. Stop pursuing your agenda for a second and let your mind settle on its own. Then look at your situation freshly and you'll know how to respond." You could call that trust.

People can discover the Zen approach intentionally or by accident. Some people find it through nature, however briefly. Others through fatigue or grief. And some might just visit a Zen center out of curiosity.

It doesn't have to be a giant experience. You can learn the fundamentals of Zen just by being quiet for a few minutes and observing what happens when you aren't fighting things, including your own inability to sit still.

When you stop fighting things, you deprive the thinking engine of gasoline. It just starts to spin a little slower, and when it does, the entire mind-made world starts to thaw out. At first you might not realize how much of your worldview is mind-made. But then you see that the stories, the dramas, the resentments, the hopelessness, it's all mind-made. The situations might not change, but their finality might. Possibility blooms. Kindness reveals itself. Wisdom starts to percolate.

The Zen approach invites you to trust that if you sit still and just wait—just wait—you'll discover a better approach to living your life than trying to think your way through it.
