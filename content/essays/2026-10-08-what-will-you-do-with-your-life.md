If you decide to take Buddhist precepts to become a dharma teacher, one of the things you have to submit is a short essay about your motivations for practice.

The easy way to answer that, and maybe the insincere way, is to just give the answer you think sounds right. "My motivations are to help others, Kwan Um means perceive world sound, Bodhisattva action, yadda yadda..." I'm not saying this answer isn't right. It's wonderful. But it's only wonderful if it's sincere.

I think it's normal for your motivations for practice (for Zen) to evolve and to be complex rather than singular.

Before I go on, take a second to reflect on these questions:

1. Why do you practice Zen today?
2. Why did you start practicing?
3. Is there something deeper that pulls you to this practice?

I started practicing Zen because I knew it was promising. I knew it was a way to actually address the things that had nagged at me: feeling closed off, feeling alone, feeling friction with myself and my surroundings, and always searching. Little did I realize that searching to escape these things are what perpetuated them.

But if I'm being honest, I'm also intrigued by Zen. The paradox that's woven into the mythology and stories is a part of it. The other part is trying to become more and more intimate with an experience that's so mysterious and delicate and nuanced. An experience that retreats when you pursue it and appears only when you give up on it entirely. Such a clever trick it performs.

In life we get the opportunity to dedicate our life to something: a vision, a problem, or even a question. What will we choose? What's worth being chosen?
