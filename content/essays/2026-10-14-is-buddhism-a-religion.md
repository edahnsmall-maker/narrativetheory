To outsiders, Buddhism might look like a religion. There are uniforms, ceremonies, temples, and a leader that people revere.

But if you ask seasoned, professional Buddhists, they'll hesitate and start squirming. Why?

Well, for one thing, when you practice Buddhism you develop a sensitivity to thinking and cogitation, and you see the role it plays in people's lives. From there it's easy to see how most religions, at their core, are about adopting a set of beliefs. If you deny those beliefs you can lose membership in the group.

Buddhism doesn't have a strict set of beliefs that one must adhere to. In fact, it often encourages you to suspend your beliefs, especially if you're practicing Zen. Buddhists don't really pray to a god, and they don't worship Buddha as a god—they revere him as a great teacher.

So what really makes someone a Buddhist?

Answering that question is tough, because who really decides who's in and who's out? There's no objective test, so we have to think about what it really means to be Buddhist and come up with a definition others would tend to agree with.

I think being Buddhist is really about an understanding—not a belief—that if we can tame our minds a bit, we can come closer to wisdom and peace. And that wisdom and peace can help us make sense of our selves and our lives. Practicing Buddhism, then, is just the strategies we use to tame the mind, and each tradition (and each yana) developed its own approach.

In Theravada Buddhism, practice involves strict ethical standards, renunciation, sutra study, and meditation. In Mahayana Buddhism, the emphasis shifted away from the individual and towards the welfare of others. In Zen Buddhism, paradox and pointing to direct experience became a way to bypass the mind's tendency to build philosophical systems and rigid ideas, and in Vajrayana Buddhism, ritual, mantras, tantras, and visualizations are introduced to help stabilize the mind and build wisdom.

If we zoom out one step further we can see that Buddha was part of a larger group of mystics and seekers across the ages who were interested in the contemplation and resolution of life's big questions:

What's going on here?

How does my life work?

What's worth pursuing?

Why is it that sometimes I feel at peace while other times I'm a miserable wreck?

Why are we here?

In classic Buddhism, a person who dedicated themselves fully to seeking these answers and started the process of awakening was known as a "stream enterer." Once entered, enlightenment was believed to be inevitable.

May we all enter the stream together and wake up, not just for ourselves but for the benefit of others as well.
