Different schools work with kong-ans (koans) differently. In my school, we keep the kong-an as a question as we sit, but we also look to integrate their lessons into our lives. I'll give you an example from one of my favorite ones. This is from Gateless Gate, kong-an #5:

"It is like a man hanging from a tree. He is holding to a branch by his teeth. His hands and his legs are all tied, so he cannot grasp another branch, and he cannot grasp the trunk of the tree. Then just at that time somebody comes and asks him "Why did Bodhidharma come to China?" If he does not answer, he is avoiding his duty and will be killed. If he opens his mouth to answer, he will fall off the tree and also die."

What can you do in that situation?

At first glance, maybe it seems hard to relate to this situation. How often do you find yourself hanging from a tree branch from your mouth? And just then someone approaches you and says "pardon me, what is Zen?"

But really this is about situations in life where both action and inaction are bad options. You're damned if you do, damned if you don't. And to me, that's the situation I find myself in often with Zen practice.

After having any kind of "good" or meaningful experience in meditation, my habitual response is to try and reproduce it. I imagine I'm not the only one. Even after years of practice, it's still my instinct: to try and recreate this experience by retracing my steps or just trying to force my mind into some kind of Zenny state.

And if I'm lucky, something short-circuits that habit. I think to myself: wait a second. What am I doing? When I had that profound experience last time, it's because I wasn't searching for anything. I wasn't trying to recreate something. It was more spaciousness than any specific thought or feeling. Trying to return to that will just end up stirring up my mind even more.

But the mind is infinitely crafty, right? So I tell myself "stop trying to get somewhere! Surrender!" But this is just another version of trying, just in the opposite direction. I'm still maintaining a goal, and that comes with judgments, evaluations, relationships, beliefs, identities—thinking, thinking, thinking.

And right then, I'm stuck. If I try, I fail. If I don't try, I fail. I'm the guy in the tree trying to express Zen.

What to do?

Wrestling with these questions in real life, to me, are some of the most important experiences we can have because they don't just teach us how to handle strangers asking us hard questions when we're dangling from branches: they teach us how to move through life with real wisdom and clarity.
